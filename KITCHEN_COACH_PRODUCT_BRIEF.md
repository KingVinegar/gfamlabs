# Project Kitchen Coach — Product Brief v0.1

## One-sentence promise

**Practice the cooking calls that recipes and cooking shows skip, before dinner depends on them.**

## Product position

A mobile, guided-practice game for home cooks who can execute instructions but want to develop real kitchen judgment. A calm coach gives the learner a realistic moment, asks what they notice and what they would do, then explains the outcome and the underlying technique.

It is deliberately not a recipe app. A recipe says *what to do*; this product rehearses *how to decide when what you planned is no longer enough*.

## The coaching model to borrow

The relevant Dr. Wolf pattern is not chess content or its exact reward system. It is the combination of:

1. **Play:** make an authentic decision in context.
2. **Coaching at the moment of need:** notice what was missed without shaming the learner.
3. **Training:** return to a skill deliberately, outside a full situation.
4. **Mistake practice:** revisit a familiar misdiagnosis later with a changed context.
5. **Progress:** show growing judgment, not merely completed content.

For cooking, the coach's voice should be observant, precise, patient, and modest: "Taste again. The flavors are present, but they are quiet. Before adding more spices, what would make those flavors easier to perceive?"

## Core loop

1. **Arrive in a situation.** The learner sees the dish, goal, time, ingredients, tools, and one or two sensory observations.
2. **Name the problem.** Use plain language first; introduce formal technique terms in the explanation.
3. **Choose an intervention.** A decision may include a single action or a short sequence.
4. **See the result and trade-off.** The coach shows what changed, what was preserved, and what became harder.
5. **Learn the why.** One short, visual explanation connects the outcome to a cooking principle.
6. **Rehearse or try it.** The learner can retry with a changed constraint, or take a tiny real-kitchen drill.

The game grades the best response **for the stated constraint**, not universal right answers. A good choice for a rushed weeknight can be a poor choice for a dinner party or a long-simmered stock.

## First vertical slice: "The Quiet Stew"

### Setup

It is 6:20 p.m. A vegetable-and-beef stew has been simmering. The texture is good, the vegetables are tender, and the aromas are appealing. On tasting, the cook says: "I like the flavors, but it tastes bland." Guests arrive at 7:00. There is salt, lemon, tomato paste, stock concentrate, herbs, and butter available.

### Learning objective

Distinguish under-seasoning from missing flavor complexity, and understand that salt can increase perceived flavor rather than merely make food taste salty.

### Decision structure

The learner first chooses the more likely diagnosis:

- The stew needs more seasoning balance.
- The stew needs more long-cooked flavor development.
- The stew needs more body.

They then choose a response. The best-fit path is to add salt gradually, stir, rest briefly, and taste again. Lemon can be a valid follow-up if the stew still needs lift; adding herbs or tomato paste may change the dish but does not directly establish whether seasoning was the missing variable. Adding stock concentrate risks oversalting and can obscure the diagnosis.

### Coaching and recovery

If the learner reaches for lemon immediately, the coach does not call it foolish: acid may brighten a dish, but first establish the seasoning baseline. The learner can still recover by tasting after each addition and avoiding a large corrective jump. The slice must demonstrate that attentive tasting is the skill, not memorizing the word "salt."

### Optional real-kitchen drill

Make a small, unsalted cup of simple soup or broth. Taste it, add a tiny measured amount of salt, taste again, then add a small amount of acid to a second spoonful. Note the difference in perceived flavor and brightness.

## Early campaign map

| Campaign | Decisions rehearsed | Example situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Balance | salt, acid, fat, dilution, concentration | A flavorful soup tastes flat; a vinaigrette is harsh. |
| Heat and browning | pan heat, crowding, timing, deglazing | Mushrooms steam instead of brown; garlic is darkening too quickly. |
| Texture and body | reduction, starch, emulsions, gelatin, water management | A sauce splits; a stew is thin; mashed potatoes turn gluey. |
| Time and constraints | sequencing, substitutions, equipment choices | Dinner is late; an ingredient is missing; a broth needs flavor fast. |
| Saves | error recognition and recovery | Over-salted soup; broken mayonnaise; scorched fond; underdone rice. |

Cuisine packs are a later extension only after the general coaching loop proves compelling. They should teach decisions in a cuisine's context, with qualified editorial input, rather than reduce a cuisine to interchangeable flavor trivia.

## Content and trust rules

- Every public scenario needs a source trail and culinary editorial review appropriate to its claim.
- Keep food-safety rules distinct from optional taste/style decisions. When a safety issue is central, state the limitation plainly; do not gamify dangerous shortcuts.
- Avoid universal claims. State conditions, tools, time, and desired result.
- Use images, audio, and tactile-feeling feedback only where they help the learner notice a real cue. Do not let production spectacle hide a weak decision.

## Commercial hypothesis

The initial paid value is a finite, authored coaching experience that builds confidence and reusable judgment. It should be evaluated as a premium core product with optional finite campaign packs, not an AI-generation or streak-driven subscription by default.

This is a hypothesis. Validation still needs to establish whether the intended audience will return for practice and pay for authored cooking scenarios.

## First validation artifact

Build a phone-sized playable prototype containing 10–15 connected scenarios from the **Balance** campaign, with a visible coach, hints, a gentle recovery path, a progress view, and one replay/mistake-practice loop. It must not require account creation, a recipe database, or external services.
